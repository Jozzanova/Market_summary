# Macro, Sunday 15 November 2026

**No data of note were released over the weekend, and the news came from central bankers. Boston Fed president Susan Collins argued for steady, gradual hikes, a view that leaves December and January intact but argues against a larger step, and Bank of England chief economist Huw Pill signalled further hikes from December. Together they confirm that the major central banks outside Japan are still tightening, with the debate about the pace rather than the direction.**

**Federal Reserve.** Susan Collins said on Saturday that "a steady, gradual path seems appropriate" at the moment, while stressing that she remains open-minded ([Reuters](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/feds-collins-says-gradual-rate-154619584.html)). She sees two-sided risks: inflation is high and broadening, the labour market is stable with potential to strengthen, and the rise in medium- and longer-term rates since the start of the year may restrain demand more than she assumes. That, she said, argues against a more aggressive pace, although the price data of the past three months and a firmer labour market make her think the path of rates over the next year "might be a bit higher" than she expected a couple of months ago. Reuters notes that a quarter-point hike on 9 December and another in January are widely expected, while last week's price data and remarks by Governor Christopher Waller suggested that some may favour a larger move. Fed funds futures price about 27 basis points for December and a further 20 basis points for January.

**Bank of England.** Huw Pill told the FT that after the hike to 4 per cent "we have further to go", that he expects to "see evidence that motivates further rises", and that the bank "waited too long" ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/nov/14/interest-rate-rises-inflation-bank-of-england-chief-economist)). He considers an end to the Iran conflict and a fall in energy prices a very low probability, worries about second-round effects in wages, and called market expectations of rates near 5 per cent in 2027 "not outrageous". With UK inflation expected to rise towards 5 per cent in the spring, the labour market figures on Tuesday and the October CPI on Wednesday ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/releasecalendar)) will test the case for a December hike; Bloomberg expects the data to show a stabilising labour market and inflation at a one-year high ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-14/boe-s-case-to-raise-rates-again-may-strengthen-on-uk-price-data)).

**Other.** The chairman of the Bank of Thailand criticised slow regulatory reform and a prolonged contraction in credit to small firms ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-15/thai-central-bank-chairman-rebukes-slow-reform-sme-credit-slump)). In Australia, auction clearance rates fell to the lowest in more than six years after the Reserve Bank's hikes ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-15/australia-auction-clearances-slump-to-six-year-low-as-rates-rise)), a sign that the 4.85 per cent policy rate is reaching the housing market, consistent with futures that price no further move on 8 December.

## Assessment

The balance of risks has not changed over the weekend. In the United States, the debate is whether the Fed hikes in steps of 25 basis points at consecutive meetings or accelerates; Collins's emphasis on the restraint from higher long-term rates suggests that the 33 basis point rise in the 5-year yield last week is itself part of the tightening. In the United Kingdom and the euro area, energy-driven inflation and the risk of second-round effects in wages dominate the arguments of policymakers. The main downside risk is the equity market: a further fall in AI shares after Nvidia's results would tighten financial conditions independently of the central banks.
